      What you are looking at

      Every creature carries a genome: body pixels, colours, size, muscle, armour, diet, the number of eyes, their field of view and range, mouth size, litter size, lifespan, and even its own mutation rate and learning rate. The genome also holds a small recurrent neural network — its brain.

      Each eye watches a slice of the world and reports four things: plants, prey or meat, threats, and kin. The brain also feels hunger, health, age, pain, a heartbeat clock, crowding, and the glow signals of others. It decides how to move and turn, when to bite, when to glow, when to share food, and when to breed.

      No behaviour is programmed. Creatures that find food survive long enough to breed; their children inherit a mutated (and, with sexual reproduction, recombined) copy of the genome. Brains can grow new neurons, wire or prune synapses. When a lineage drifts far enough from its ancestors it becomes a new species, keeping its genus name.

      Watch for emergence: herds forming, packs of predators hunting together (bites from kin stack), creatures sharing food with starving relatives, night-time glow languages, arms races between armour and jaws, and brains getting bigger when you lower the brain cost.

      Controls
